ep. 1 A Vision of the Future

November 3, 2026

Advances in artificial intelligence and robotics will dramatically increase humanity's productive capacity. But an increase in productive capacity does not necessarily lead to greater prosperity for people. On the contrary, in a society where human labor is no longer needed, most people may lose both their economic foundations and their social roles.

AI already performs some forms of intellectual work, and I expect it to rapidly replace people in a wide range of fields, including research and development and software development. Later, as robotics advances sufficiently and becomes widely available, much of the physical labor in manufacturing, logistics, and service industries will also be automated. These changes may create new jobs, but the pace of job creation is likely to fall short of the pace at which existing jobs disappear.

What concerns me most in this process is the gap between productive capacity and purchasing power. Wealth will be concentrated among companies and asset owners who own AI and automated systems, while most people who depend on labor will lose their income. Even if technological progress lowers the cost of producing goods and services, consumption will inevitably contract if incomes fall faster. Falling consumption will, in turn, reduce corporate revenues and employment, potentially trapping society as a whole in a prolonged slump in demand.

Economic changes will also have a profound effect on social structures. People who find it difficult to support themselves financially will become more dependent on their families and reduce investment in education and the future. But a more fundamental problem is that people will find it increasingly difficult to find a role and a sense of meaning in society.

People do not work merely to survive. They live by being needed by others, contributing to society, and influencing one another. As the need for labor and collaboration declines, opportunities for these experiences may also diminish. Rather than continuing to compete, people may withdraw from social participation and gradually become isolated. Even within families, greater economic dependence does not necessarily deepen bonds; it may instead intensify conflict and emotional estrangement.

I believe that many of these changes could emerge over the next ten years.

  • 2026–2029: Automation of intellectual work by AI and a decline in new hiring
  • 2028–2031: A downturn in the domestic economy as household incomes fall and consumption contracts
  • 2030–2034: The substantial replacement of physical labor as robotics becomes more widespread
  • 2033–2036: The possibility that economic polarization, social isolation, and the weakening of communities become structurally entrenched

Of course, this future is not inevitable. The pace of technological progress and its actual adoption remain uncertain, and entirely different outcomes could emerge depending on government redistribution policies, new income structures, and how technology is owned. With an adequate social response, people could be freed from labor and enjoy more prosperous lives.

My focus, however, is not the best future that technology could create, but the future that may emerge when social institutions fail to keep pace with technological progress.

Even in a society where the problem of production has been solved, the problem of distribution remains. And even if distribution is resolved, questions of belonging and meaning will remain.

The crucial question, then, is this.

What kind of society should we build when human beings are no longer needed?

ep. 2 Fortuna Favors the Bold.

November 3, 2026

I have decided to act in response to the future I envision.

I could aim simply to survive 'well' myself, but what I want is to help the people around me live well too.

My goal is to be a small but steady source of support in people's lives. I am thinking about a basic business that could directly produce things people need, without depending on external circumstances.

There are, of course, a few problems. The biggest is that other people and I are not on the same page about this vision of the future.

My view of the future is admittedly extreme in some respects. Still, I feel frustrated when I see people being deeply complacent or looking to history for answers on the assumption that the future will unfold much as it always has.

Second, I do not have the energy or courage to turn my ideas into reality as I imagine them. I keep saying that we need to do something to prepare for what lies ahead, but in truth I am not so different: I have done nothing to prepare either.

Perhaps this is just an excuse, but I already feel a little late. Because of graduate school entrance exams. Of course, even if I feel late, this is still the earliest moment I can start.

For now, all I can rely on is Fortuna, the goddess of fortune who loves me.

It is irrational to have to believe in her first, regardless of whether she actually exists, but I thought I should move forward anyway.

I am too old to spend my time caught in melancholy and trying to soothe my loneliness, yet still too young to stand still.

ep. 3 Why I Want to Study Mathematics

November 3, 2026

I find mathematics to be a very beautiful discipline, but I think mathematical research has immediate practical value less often than research in other fields. For example, even if a problem such as the Goldbach conjecture were suddenly solved one day, our everyday lives would probably change very little.

For that reason, I understand that people have different views on the value of mathematical research. Yet I think it is precisely this characteristic that makes possible a highly constructive culture in which discovered truths are shared with everyone, much like programmers' open-source projects. I also find it appealing that once a truth is discovered, it never changes. I value these qualities highly, and I wonder whether the joy mathematicians feel as mathematics advances might itself be more than enough to give it value.

This word, 'joy,' is difficult to pin down. When people talk about joy, they might mean many things: making a lot of money, hearing amusing and entertaining stories, or beating others in a competition. But the joy of mathematics does not really feel like any of those things to me.

As an analogy, doing mathematical research is like continually building an enormous sandcastle on Earth. Some people might build small structures, others large ones, while still others add supporting details apart from the structures themselves. Everyone works together, finding a sense of fulfillment in watching it gradually take shape.

As someone who dreams of living as a mathematician, AI is something I have recently been deeply interested in and fascinated by, while also remaining wary of it. Given the tremendous growth of AI technology today, I expect the mathematical community of the future to feel quite different from the one we know now.

If humans provide the key ideas and important problems, AI may solve relatively easy problems at great speed, allowing people to tackle the most valuable problems across a range of fields more directly. Demand for a wide variety of techniques will also grow rapidly, so large-scale collaboration may become essential.

Even if the future of mathematical research as a profession becomes uncertain, I think I will find great joy in studying mathematics and discovering and understanding new truths. I chose to major in mathematics because that is the life I want to continue living.

For the sake of others who study mathematics with a similar spirit, I always keep the possibility of a crisis in mind, and I will do everything I can to support the advancement of mathematics.
